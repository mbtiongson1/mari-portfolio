Healthcare Documentation Information Architecture

Knowledge Base Article Redesign

Restructuring a high-traffic KB article serving healthcare professionals. The original article had the right information but the wrong structure, leading to repeated support escalations on questions the article already answered.

The Artifact

What the final article looks like

Below is a redacted excerpt from the restructured KB article. Company names, proprietary processes, and identifying details have been replaced, but the information architecture is preserved.

Redacted KB Article Excerpt

Enrollment Verification Process

This article covers the steps for verifying member enrollment status in ██████████. Use this process when a member contacts support about enrollment discrepancies.

Before You Begin

  • Confirm you have access to ████████ (Level 2 or above)
  • Have the member's ██████ ID ready
  • Check the member's enrollment window (see ████████████ for current dates)

Step-by-Step Verification

1. Access the member record. Navigate to ██████████ → Member Search → enter the ██████ ID. The enrollment status displays in the header bar.

2. Verify enrollment tier. Compare the displayed tier against the member's reported tier. If they match, proceed to Step 4. If they don't match, this is an enrollment discrepancy.

3. For discrepancies: Check the enrollment history tab for recent changes. Changes made within the last ██ days may not yet be reflected.

4. Confirm and document. Log the verification in ██████████ with resolution code ████.

The Process

How I thought through the redesign

The original article was written by a subject matter expert who understood the enrollment system deeply. That was the problem: the article assumed the reader shared that understanding. It opened with a system architecture overview, buried the actual steps in the middle, and ended with edge cases that applied to fewer than 5% of inquiries.

I started by pulling support ticket data. Of the escalations tagged to this article, 73% were from agents who had opened the article, scrolled through it, and still couldn't find the answer. The information was there. The structure wasn't serving it.

The core question

What does the reader need to do, and in what order do they need to learn it? Not: what does the reader need to know about how the system works.

I restructured around the reader's actual workflow, not the system's architecture. The "Before You Begin" section was new. The original article scattered prerequisites across three different paragraphs. Consolidating them meant agents could self-check readiness before starting, which cut the "I don't have access" escalations significantly.

Decision

Moved the system architecture overview to a linked reference article instead of deleting it. SMEs valued it for training; it just didn't belong in a task-oriented article.

Decision

Used "If they match, proceed to Step 4" branching instead of nested sub-steps. Testing showed agents processed linear steps with conditional skips faster than tree-structured procedures.

The language changes were subtle but intentional. I replaced "the system will display" (passive, describes the system) with "the enrollment status displays" (active, describes what the agent sees). Every sentence answers "what do I do next?" or "what am I looking at?"

Stakeholder alignment took longer than the writing. The SME who authored the original article had strong opinions about the architecture overview. Rather than cutting their work, I proposed the linked reference article as a compromise that preserved their expertise in a context where it would be more useful.

What I learned

Documentation redesigns are rarely about better writing. They're about understanding the gap between what the author knows and what the reader needs, then building a bridge. The writing is the bridge; the thinking is the surveying.

The Impact

What changed

Support escalations tagged to this article dropped 41% in the first month after the redesign. The average time agents spent on the article before resolving a ticket decreased from 4.2 minutes to 1.8 minutes. The linked reference article received steady traffic from agents in training, suggesting the SME's architecture content found its right audience.
